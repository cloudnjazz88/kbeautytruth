I have dry, somewhat sensitive skin, and I currently use The Ordinary Niacinamide 10% + Zinc 1% as one example of a 10% formula. For the long-term product notes on that bottle, see [my The Ordinary Niacinamide 10% + Zinc 1% review](/reviews/the-ordinary-niacinamide-10-zinc-1-review/). ## What is niacinamide?

Niacinamide is a form of vitamin B3 used widely in skincare. It shows up in serums, moisturizers, and treatments because it is versatile and, for many people, comfortable to use for everyday goals like tone and texture.

It is still a cosmetic ingredient, not a medicine. One product will not solve every skin concern on its own.

## What does niacinamide do for skin?

Topical niacinamide is studied for several skin-appearance benefits. Results can vary by formula, concentration, and the person using it.

**Uneven-looking tone.** Clinical work, often around 4% to 5%, has reported improvements in the look of hyperpigmented spots and overall tone compared with control formulas. That is about how skin looks, not a medical treatment for pigmentation.

**Texture.** Studies have also reported improvements in the appearance of fine lines and skin texture with topical niacinamide.

**Barrier and hydration support.** Niacinamide has been studied for supporting the skin barrier and reducing transepidermal water loss in some settings, including research on ceramides and other stratum corneum lipids. That helps explain why many people find it comfortable in a routine.

**Oil and the look of pores.** Some research on 2% niacinamide reported reductions in sebum measures in certain groups. Pores are not doors that open and close. When oil and the skin surface look more settled, pores can simply look less noticeable.

Niacinamide can support the appearance of tone and texture. It is not a cure for acne, wrinkles, or pigmentation, and it is not a permanent fix.

## Niacinamide concentrations: is 10% better?

In research and many cosmetic formulas, niacinamide often shows up in the **2% to 5%** range. Reviews of cosmetic use also note that about **4% to 5%** is a common bracket for tone and aging-appearance studies. A 10% product is not automatically too strong. Plenty of people use 10% without a problem, and I am one of them so far.

A higher percentage also does not automatically mean better results. The full formula, how often you use it, and how your skin tolerates it matter as much as the number on the label. If your skin is easily irritated, starting with a lower concentration or using a 10% product less often can be a practical way to see how you respond. That is about pacing, not about treating a higher percentage as off-limits.

## Can sensitive skin use niacinamide?

Niacinamide is often described as well tolerated, and many people with reactive skin use it without trouble. That is not the same as saying it is non-irritating for everyone.

If you get ongoing stinging, redness, dryness, or irritation, it is reasonable to use the product less often or stop. Patch testing a new product and introducing it slowly are still sensible habits, especially if your barrier already feels compromised. None of that replaces advice from a clinician when something feels seriously off.

## Can you use niacinamide with retinol?

I used to think niacinamide and retinol should never be used together. After looking into it more carefully, I learned that this is not a universal rule.

In general, niacinamide and retinol can be used in the same routine. They are not ingredients that cancel each other out, and there is no solid basis for an absolute “never mix them” rule. Some regimens studied in dermatology research include niacinamide alongside retinoid-related ingredients, and niacinamide’s barrier-supporting properties may help some people use retinol more comfortably.

What *can* get uncomfortable is irritation burden: using several actives at once can be a lot for dry or sensitive skin, even when the ingredients are compatible on paper. Because I use a 10% niacinamide product, I no longer treat the combination as off-limits, but I still pay attention to how much my skin is handling when I layer active products. Alternating or spacing them is a personal tolerance choice, not a universal ban.

If you are new to retinol, my [retinol for beginners](/ingredients/retinol-for-beginners/) note covers a cautious pace. In [my morning and evening routine](/routines/), I also decide actives based on how my skin feels rather than forcing every step every night.

## How to use niacinamide

A common order is cleanser, then toner if you use one, then a niacinamide treatment, then moisturizer. Many people use it morning, night, or both. In the morning, finish with sunscreen.

Product texture can change the order. “Thinner before thicker” is a useful default, not a law. A niacinamide moisturizer already sits in the cream step; a watery serum usually goes earlier.

One practical tip: check ingredient lists. Niacinamide shows up in a lot of formulas now, so you may already be using more than one product that contains it. That does not automatically mean you should stop, but it helps explain why your skin might feel overloaded if you add another strong treatment on top.

## Kay’s take

I started niacinamide for overall tone and texture, and the 10% product I use has suited my skin well. My skin looked noticeably smoother and more even, with the almost polished look Koreans sometimes describe as “peeled egg” skin. I did not get stinging, redness, breakouts, or other irritation from it.

That is my experience only. It does not mean everyone needs 10%, or that 10% is the best choice for every skin type. Finding a concentration and frequency your skin accepts still matters more than matching a number on a label.
