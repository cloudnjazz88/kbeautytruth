Retinol can be a useful skincare ingredient, but it is easy to get impatient with it. When you do not see a change right away, applying more or using it more often can feel like the way to speed things up. My family learned why that can be a mistake.

I have dry, somewhat sensitive skin. I use The Ordinary Retinol 0.2% in Squalane in my evening routine about once every three days. So far, I have not had irritation, and my skin feels a little firmer to me. That is my impression, not proof that retinol alone caused the change.

## What can retinol do?

Retinol is a form of vitamin A used in over-the-counter skincare for concerns such as uneven texture, fine lines, and acne. It sits in the broader retinoid family with stronger prescription options such as tretinoin and oral isotretinoin, but a cosmetic retinol serum is not the same product, strength, or exposure as a prescription retinoid. Results are not immediate, and a product that works for one person may feel too irritating for another.

That delay is where it gets tricky. Not seeing a result after a few uses does not mean you need a thicker layer or a stronger product.

## Why I think beginners should start slowly

Retinol can leave skin dry, red, stinging, or peeling. I think it makes sense to begin with a lower-strength product and give your skin time to show you how it responds. But the percentage is only part of the picture: **how much you apply and how often you apply it matter too.** Even a lower percentage is not a guarantee against irritation.

For my own skin, using retinol about every three days has been a comfortable pace. I follow with moisturizer and do not increase my use just because I want to see a change sooner. That is my experience, not a rule that everyone needs to follow.

## What happened when my aunt first tried retinol

I gave my aunt a retinol product that was labeled 0.3%. She had never used retinol before. When she did not notice an effect right away, she began using roughly four or five pumps every day.

Her skin became badly irritated and started peeling, almost like the peeling she associated with a sunburn. She stopped using the product, and it took about three weeks for her skin to recover.

I do not know how much each pump dispensed, and I cannot separate the effects of the amount, the daily use, the formula, or the rest of her routine. I also would not assume everyone would react the same way. What I do know is that she was new to retinol, used a lot of it every day, and her skin reacted badly.

The story did not end there. Once her skin had recovered, she tried using retinol about once every three days instead. She tells me she is happy with how she uses it now. To me, that is the useful lesson: the answer was not necessarily to use *more*. It was to find a pace her skin could handle.

## What if your skin is reacting?

If your skin is getting increasingly uncomfortable, red, or visibly peeling, do not treat that as a sign to push through with more retinol. Pause it and keep the rest of your routine gentle. For a severe reaction, or one that does not improve, speak with a healthcare professional.

I also make room for moisturizer in my routine and use sun protection during the day. Retinol does not replace those basics.

## Pregnancy, trying to conceive, and breastfeeding

This is not personalized medical advice. If you are pregnant, trying to conceive, or breastfeeding, ask your own clinician before using retinol or other retinoids.

Authoritative clinical sources treat retinoids cautiously in pregnancy. The American Academy of Dermatology advises avoiding retinoids while pregnant, including prescription options and over-the-counter retinol products. ACOG similarly notes that topical retinoids are in the same vitamin A family as oral isotretinoin, that systemic absorption from topical use is generally low, and that avoidance during pregnancy is still generally recommended. Oral isotretinoin is a separate, high-risk prescription medicine and is not the same exposure as a cosmetic topical retinol serum.

I am not making a categorical claim about breastfeeding here. Medication decisions while breastfeeding belong with your clinician and up-to-date clinical references.

## Kay’s take

I think retinol is worth considering, but patience is part of using it well. I use a low-strength product about every three days because that has worked comfortably for my dry, somewhat sensitive skin. My aunt’s experience reminds me why I do not want to turn “I cannot see results yet” into “I should use more tonight.”

Start cautiously. Pay attention to how your skin feels. You can decide whether to adjust your routine later, but you cannot rush past irritation and expect your skin to thank you for it.
