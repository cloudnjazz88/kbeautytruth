An exfoliating toner can look like a very small addition to a routine. Then you notice another bottle with a different acid, a pad that also exfoliates, and a serum promising smoother skin. It becomes surprisingly easy to add the same kind of work several times.

Salicylic acid and glycolic acid are two names worth separating before that happens. Both are used for exfoliation, but choosing between them is more useful than collecting both by default.

This is an ingredient guide, not a side-by-side test on my skin. I would start with the concern I want to address, then look at the complete product and the rest of the routine.

The concerns overlap. This table is a starting point for reading products, rather than a rule that assigns an acid to every skin type.

## What salicylic acid does

The [American Academy of Dermatology](https://www.aad.org/public/diseases/acne/diy/adult-acne-treatment) describes salicylic acid as an ingredient that exfoliates and helps unclog pores. Its adult-acne guidance particularly identifies blackheads and whiteheads as concerns it can address.

That gives it a clearer purpose than simply chasing a shinier finish. It also does not mean it treats every kind of breakout, or that every salicylic-acid cosmetic is an acne medication.

The [FDA’s BHA page](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/beta-hydroxy-acids) identifies salicylic acid as the BHA commonly used in cosmetics. On a US acne treatment, read the Drug Facts panel as well as the ingredient list. A cosmetic toner and an acne treatment can have different instructions even when the ingredient name is familiar.

## What glycolic acid does

Glycolic acid belongs to the AHA family. The [FDA explains](https://www.fda.gov/cosmetics/cosmetic-ingredients/alpha-hydroxy-acids) that AHA products exfoliate the skin’s surface. Products in this family are marketed for concerns including texture and tone, but that positioning does not guarantee a particular result from every bottle.

Glycolic acid is therefore worth understanding when comparing surface-exfoliating products. It is not an instruction to peel away anything that looks dull. A moisturizer and an acid are different decisions, even if both are advertised with the word glow.

## The percentage is only part of the product

A larger number on one bottle does not make it the better choice. Nor can you directly compare the percentages of two different acids as though they were a single strength scale.

For AHAs, the FDA notes that exfoliation depends on acid type, concentration, pH, and the other ingredients. That is a good reason to read beyond the headline number.

I would also check whether the product is rinsed off or left on, where the brand directs it to be used, and how it fits the existing routine. A cleanser, pad, serum, and peel should not be treated as interchangeable instructions.

## For slightly dry, slightly sensitive skin

I would not choose an acid simply because a chart says AHA is for dry skin or BHA is for oily skin. Those shortcuts leave out the actual concern and the formula.

The [AAD’s exfoliation guidance](https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/skin-care-secrets/routine/safely-exfoliate-at-home) notes that dry or sensitive skin may find mechanical exfoliation irritating. It also warns that adding exfoliation to products such as retinol or benzoyl peroxide can worsen dryness or irritation.

That matters with pads as well as liquids. A textured pad adds an application method to the formula, and repeated rubbing is another decision. My [toner-pad guide](/trends/what-are-toner-pads/) explains why a moisturizing mini mask and an exfoliating swipe should not be treated as the same step.

If skin is already irritated, another exfoliant is not where I would begin. The AAD advises against exfoliating skin with open wounds or sunburn and recommends moisturizing after exfoliation. My [ceramides and hyaluronic acid guide](/ingredients/ceramides-vs-hyaluronic-acid/) looks at hydration and barrier support separately from exfoliation.

## Can you use both?

Some products combine acids. That does not establish that two separate products belong together in your routine, or that a combined formula will be comfortable for your skin.

Before adding another one, count what is already there: the exfoliating cleanser, the pore pad, the acid toner, and any other treatment. Different packaging can hide a repeated step.

I would avoid prescribing a fixed weekly schedule from ingredient names alone. Follow the particular product’s directions and consider the other treatments you use. Persistent irritation or acne deserves individualized advice rather than a stronger shopping list.

## Sun protection belongs in the decision

The FDA advises sunscreen, protective clothing, and limiting sun exposure while using AHA products and for a week after stopping, because they may increase sun sensitivity. Moving glycolic acid to the evening does not remove that guidance.

Choosing salicylic acid is not a reason to skip sun protection either. The FDA’s BHA guidance also advises sun protection with these products.

## What I would choose by

For blackheads or whiteheads, salicylic acid has a specific role to investigate. For a surface-exfoliating product aimed at texture, glycolic acid is one option to understand.

For skin that mostly feels dry or uncomfortable, I would first ask whether exfoliation is actually the missing step. The most useful bottle is the one with a clear job in the routine and directions you can follow comfortably. It does not have to be the strongest one, and it does not need a second acid beside it.
